# French Bulldog Colors & Genetics: What Buyers Should Know

> **Editorial draft — not published.** This copy is source-grounded but still needs the owner's
> verified photos, redacted reports, breeder-specific facts, and final medical/genetics review
> before website implementation.

You open a puppy listing and find a label long enough to need punctuation:

> “Lilac-and-tan fluffy carrier, testable cocoa, no pied.”

It sounds precise. It may even be based on a real DNA report. But it still leaves the most
important buyer questions unanswered.

What does the puppy actually look like? Which variants did the laboratory test? Is the label a
breed-standard term or seller shorthand? And where are the parent health screenings, puppy
veterinary records, and contract?

The easiest way to understand French Bulldog color genetics is not to memorize every letter. It
is to sort every claim into four layers: what you can see, what the lab detected, what the listing
calls it, and what the health evidence proves.

## The Four-Layer Color Decoder

Every color claim becomes easier to understand when you ask four separate questions.

| Layer             | The question                           | What it tells you                                                                                               |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1. Appearance** | What can I see?                        | The visible color, pattern, nose pigment, eye appearance, and coat length—the phenotype.                        |
| **2. Laboratory** | Which variants were actually tested?   | Part of the dog's genotype, limited to the genes and variants included in that test.                            |
| **3. Label**      | What does the seller call it?          | Sometimes a breed-standard term; sometimes breeder shorthand or a marketing name.                               |
| **4. Evidence**   | What protects this puppy beyond color? | Parent screening, pairing decisions, veterinary records, temperament, structure, contract, and breeder support. |

A long label can provide useful coat information. It cannot jump from Layer 3 directly to Layer 4.

That distinction matters because French Bulldog buyers are often shown color results under the
larger heading of “DNA tested.” A coat-color panel and a health panel may appear on the same
report, but they answer different questions.

## Behind the Palette: Two Pigments and Many Controls

Most canine coat-color explanations begin with two pigment families:

- **Eumelanin** is the dark-pigment family. Genes can influence whether it appears black, brown,
  or diluted to a paler shade.
- **Pheomelanin** is the red/yellow-pigment family. Its intensity can range from pale cream to
  deeper fawn or red.

That does not mean dogs have only a two-gene paint box. Other genes help control where pigment
appears, whether a dark mask shows, whether white spotting interrupts the color, whether pigment
is diluted, and whether the coat is short or long.

The [UC Davis Veterinary Genetics Laboratory French Bulldog panel](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/panel/dog-coat-color-french-bulldog)
includes several of these controls. Here is the buyer version—not a breeding calculator.

| Test shorthand       | Think of it as                     | What a buyer should remember                                                                               |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **A locus / ASIP**   | The pattern selector               | Helps determine the placement of red/yellow and dark pigment, but other loci can override what you see.    |
| **K locus / CBD103** | The dark-pigment gate              | Influences whether dominant dark pigment or an agouti pattern can be expressed.                            |
| **E locus / MC1R**   | The pigment-production switchboard | Influences masks and whether dark pigment can appear in the coat. Multiple alleles and interactions exist. |
| **B locus / TYRP1**  | Brown pigment                      | Changes black eumelanin toward brown; it does not simply lighten every pigment in the dog.                 |
| **Co locus / HPS3**  | Cocoa                              | A separate recessive cause of brown coat identified in French Bulldogs.                                    |
| **D locus / MLPH**   | Dilution                           | Can lighten dark pigment—for example, black toward the gray shade commonly called blue.                    |
| **S locus / MITF**   | White spotting                     | Contributes to piebald or white spotting, but the amount and placement of white are variable.              |
| **PMEL**             | Merle pattern                      | Produces irregular areas of diluted and solid pigment; expression can be complex or visually hidden.       |
| **L locus / FGF5**   | Coat length                        | Recessive variants can produce the long-haired coat called fluffy. This is coat type, not color.           |

Even pigment intensity is more complicated than one universal “light” or “dark” switch. One
peer-reviewed study found five loci that together explained more than 70% of red/yellow intensity
variation in its validation group—not 100%. That is a useful reminder that an exact shade is not
always predictable from a single result.

## Standard, Registrable, and Marketed Are Not the Same Thing

These three ideas often get folded into one sentence. They should remain separate.

### What the American breed standard accepts

The current
[French Bull Dog Club of America breed standard](https://frenchbulldogclub.org/breedstandard/)
accepts white, cream, and fawn from light fawn to red fawn, plus combinations of those colors. It
also describes brindle, piebald, black masks, black shading, and white markings.

The same standard lists all other colors, markings, or patterns as disqualifications and gives
examples including solid black, black and tan, black and white, white with black, blue, blue fawn,
liver, and merle.

That is a conformation classification. It tells you how the dog compares with the written
standard used in the show ring. It is not a medical test, and a standard-colored dog is not
automatically a healthy dog.

### What AKC registration means

[AKC's own conformation FAQ](https://www.akc.org/sports/conformation/resources/faq/) explains that
a dog of a color disqualified by its breed standard may still be registered. National breed clubs
determine both conformation standards and the color choices available for registration, but those
systems are not identical.

So an AKC registration does not, by itself, mean:

- the color is accepted in the conformation standard;
- the marketing name is genetically verified;
- the parents completed the recommended health screenings; or
- the puppy has a lifetime health guarantee.

Registration answers a registration question. The lab report answers only the tests on that
report. Health records must answer the health question.

### What a marketing label means

Names such as `lilac`, `isabella`, and `platinum` are widely used in seller and breeder
vocabulary, but they are not all official terms in the FBDCA standard and their practical use may
vary. If a label affects the puppy's price or is presented as a precise genetic claim, ask for the
definition and the report behind it.

Here is the safest way to read several common terms:

- **Blue** usually refers to diluted dark pigment associated with D-locus variants. It does not
  mean the coat contains blue pigment.
- **Chocolate or brown** may involve `TYRP1` variants at the B locus, but **cocoa** is associated
  with a separate `HPS3` variant in French Bulldogs.
- **Lilac or isabella** is commonly used for combinations involving brown-family and dilution
  variants. Because labels and panels vary, the underlying results matter more than the name.
- **Platinum** is a seller term often applied to a very pale or cream-looking dog with other color
  variants carried underneath the visible coat. Ask what the seller means and which loci support
  the description.
- **Merle** is a pattern associated with `PMEL`, not simply a shade.
- **Fluffy** means long-haired. UC Davis identifies recessive `FGF5` variants behind long coat and
  specifically notes the French Bulldog nickname. Fluffy is not a color.

This is where the Four-Layer Decoder helps: record the visible phenotype, read the tested
genotype, translate the label, and then move on to the separate health evidence.

## Where Color and Health Really Do Intersect

It would be easy to replace marketing hype with an equally inaccurate warning that every
non-standard color is unhealthy. Genetics does not support a claim that broad.

The responsible approach is narrower: identify the specific variant or pairing, examine what the
research actually found, and say what remains uncertain.

### Merle: the pairing matters

[UC Davis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/merle) describes merle as an incompletely dominant pattern
associated with an insertion in the `PMEL` gene. Merle expression occurs on a continuum. Some
dogs show dramatic patches; some cryptic merles show little or no obvious pattern; mosaic results
can further complicate what appears on the coat.

That complexity is why appearance alone should not be used to plan a pairing where merle may be
present.

When two dogs carrying merle variants are paired, double-merle offspring may result. UC Davis
notes that double-merle dogs may have auditory, eye, skeletal, and other defects. A BAER study
across multiple merle breeds also found higher deafness rates among double-merle than single-merle
dogs. Its percentages should not be presented as French Bulldog-specific because the study was
not limited to Frenchies.

**What we know:** Merle testing is important when merle is present, and avoiding a merle-to-merle
pairing addresses a documented risk.

**What we cannot conclude:** Every single-merle dog is blind, deaf, or unhealthy.

**What to ask:** Were the relevant parents tested for merle, can you see the reports, and how did
the breeder evaluate the pairing?

### Dilution: a risk is not a diagnosis

Variants in `MLPH` are associated with dilute coat color. Color-dilution alopecia, or CDA, is a
recognized skin and hair disorder reported in some dilute dogs. It can involve hair thinning,
fragile hair, and secondary skin problems.

But dilute color and CDA are not interchangeable diagnoses. Not every dilute dog develops the
condition, and much of the published incidence data comes from breeds other than French Bulldogs.

**What we know:** Dilute pigmentation can be associated with CDA in some dogs.

**What we cannot conclude:** Every blue or dilute French Bulldog will develop alopecia—or that a
coat-color result can replace a veterinary skin examination.

**What to ask:** Which D-locus variants were included, is there a known skin-history pattern in the
parents and close relatives, and what has the veterinarian observed?

### White spotting and hearing: use breed-specific caution

Veterinary literature has associated congenital sensorineural deafness in several breeds with
pigmentation pathways involving white spotting and merle. The mechanism and prevalence are not
the same in every breed.

That evidence does not justify telling a buyer that every white or piebald French Bulldog is deaf.
It does justify treating hearing as a real clinical question rather than trying to diagnose it
from a photograph. When there is a specific concern, ask what hearing evaluation was performed;
do not assume a BAER test exists unless the breeder can show it.

### Cocoa: a useful lesson in scientific uncertainty

A variant in `HPS3` has been identified as a cause of the French Bulldog color called cocoa. A
small peer-reviewed study found a measurable change in platelet dense granules in cocoa dogs but
did not find evidence of major health problems or owner-reported increased bleeding in its study
group. The authors still said more research was needed to rule out subtle visual effects or a
clinically relevant bleeding disorder.

That does not prove cocoa is dangerous. It also does not prove that every possible health effect
has been ruled out. It shows what responsible interpretation looks like:

> The color-causing variant is known; the complete long-term clinical meaning is not settled by a
> single small study.

The study also demonstrates why one color result is not the whole coat story. `HPS3` interacted
with several other color genes, and HPS3 testing alone could not reliably predict the complete
phenotype.

## What a Color DNA Report Can—and Cannot—Tell You

A useful report should let you identify:

- the dog or puppy connected to the sample;
- the laboratory and report date;
- the genes or variants included in the panel;
- which variants were detected;
- whether the result describes an expressed trait, a carrier state, or no tested copy; and
- whether additional testing is needed to interpret a pairing.

A color report can help explain coat traits and inform breeding decisions for the variants it
tests. It cannot, by itself, prove that:

- every possible color variant was tested;
- the puppy will never develop a health problem;
- the parents passed hip, patella, cardiac, or ophthalmology screening;
- a visually similar dog has the same genotype; or
- a marketing name has one universal definition.

The [AKC French Bulldog DNA page](https://www.akc.org/breeder-programs/dna/dna-resource-center/akc-dna-health/breed-specific-dna-testing-recommendations/french-bulldog-dna-tests/)
illustrates this separation. It lists coat loci and other traits apart from health variants, and
then separately lists the breed club's recommended screening categories: patella, hip, congenital
cardiac, and ophthalmologist evaluations. Juvenile cataract DNA and thyroid testing are listed as
optional there.

The [OFA CHIC program](https://ofa.org/chic-programs/) can make breed-specific screening results
easier to verify. A CHIC number means the dog completed the required categories for that breed and
the results are public. It does not mean every result was normal, so review the actual record—not
only the badge or number.

## Ask for the Color Receipt

Imagine the puppy's color claim came with a receipt. Before the word `rare` adds value or urgency,
check what is actually on it.

```text
COLOR RECEIPT

[ ] The dog or puppy identity matches the report
[ ] The laboratory and report date are visible
[ ] The tested loci are listed—not just “DNA tested”
[ ] The relevant parent results are available
[ ] The pairing explanation is clear, especially for merle
[ ] Breed-specific health screenings are shown separately
[ ] The actual OFA/CHIC results were reviewed, when available
[ ] Puppy veterinary records and the contract are available
[ ] Price is not justified only by the word “rare”
```

If the label is more detailed than the evidence, slow down. A responsible answer may be, “Here is
the report, here is what it covers, and here is what it cannot tell us.” That answer is more useful
than false certainty.

## Health Before Color: A 60-Second Decision Path

Loving a particular color is not a character flaw. Color can be part of your preference. It just
should not be allowed to skip the rest of the decision.

```text
Do I like this puppy's color?
        ↓
Can the seller document the color claim?
        ↓
Can the seller separately document parent health screening
and puppy veterinary care?
        ↓
Does the pairing avoid a known high-risk color combination?
        ↓
Do temperament, structure, contract, support, and family fit
also make sense?
```

If the answer becomes vague at the health-evidence step, another color explanation will not fix
the gap.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What colors are accepted by the French Bulldog breed standard?

The current FBDCA standard accepts white, cream, fawn from light to red, and combinations of those
colors. It also describes brindle, piebald, black masks, black shading, and white markings. Read
the linked standard for its complete wording and current disqualifications.

### Can a non-standard-color French Bulldog be AKC registered?

Yes, in some cases. AKC states that a dog of a color disqualified by its breed standard may still
be registered. Registration and eligibility under the conformation color standard are separate
questions.

### Are rare-colored French Bulldogs less healthy?

`Rare` is not a medical diagnosis or a standardized genetic category. Some specific variants or
pairings have documented concerns, while other broad claims are not supported. Evaluate the exact
genotype, pairing, breed-specific screenings, family history, veterinary findings, and individual
dog.

### What is the difference between a color panel and a health panel?

A color panel looks for selected variants related to coat color, pattern, or type. A health panel
looks for selected disease-associated variants. Clinical screenings such as hip, patella,
cardiac, and ophthalmology evaluations are separate again. Always ask what was actually tested.

### Is fluffy a French Bulldog color?

No. Fluffy is a common name for the long-haired coat phenotype associated with recessive `FGF5`
variants. It describes coat length, not coat color. The FBDCA standard calls for a short, smooth
coat.

### What should I ask before buying a merle French Bulldog?

Ask to see the relevant merle test reports, confirm the dogs attached to those reports, and ask
how the pairing avoided two merle-bearing parents. Then review the separate health screenings and
puppy veterinary records just as you would for any other French Bulldog.

## The Bottom Line

A coat label can be accurate, interesting, and still be only one part of the puppy.

Read the label. Verify the report. Understand whether the term belongs to the breed standard,
laboratory vocabulary, or seller shorthand. Then give more weight to the records that color cannot
replace: parent health screening, veterinary care, responsible pairing, temperament, structure,
contract, and ongoing support.

The best color decision is an informed one. The best puppy decision is bigger than color.
