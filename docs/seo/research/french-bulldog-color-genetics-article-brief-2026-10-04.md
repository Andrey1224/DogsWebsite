# French Bulldog Colors & Genetics — Research and Article Brief

**Prepared:** October 4, 2026  
**Working title:** `French Bulldog Colors & Genetics: What Buyers Should Know`  
**Status:** Research and editorial plan only. This is not yet the publishable article.  
**Primary audience:** A prospective French Bulldog buyer who has encountered labels such as
`blue`, `lilac`, `cocoa`, `isabella`, `platinum`, `merle`, or `fluffy` and wants to understand what
those labels do—and do not—say about the dog.

## Editorial decision

This article should not be another color chart or a sales page for “rare” puppies. Its useful and
original job is to teach a buyer how to decode a color claim without confusing appearance,
genotype, breed-standard status, and health.

The central message is:

> A coat label can describe what a dog looks like or which variants a laboratory detected. It
> cannot replace breed-specific health screening, veterinary records, or evaluation of the dog in
> front of you.

The article should be accurate enough to earn trust but simple enough that a buyer does not need
to memorize allele notation. It must avoid both extremes:

- `Every non-standard color is unhealthy` is too broad and unsupported.
- `Color has nothing to do with health` is also too broad because specific pigment variants and
  breeding combinations can have documented health implications.

## Why this topic deserves a separate article

The current article library already covers general DNA health testing in
`/blog/choose-healthy-bulldog-puppy-health-tests`. That page correctly tells buyers that DNA
testing is only one layer of health review. It does not explain coat genetics or the language used
to sell particular French Bulldog colors.

This new article should extend—not repeat—the Healthy Puppy Guide:

- the Healthy Puppy Guide remains the source for general parent health tests, veterinary checks,
  and buyer records;
- this article explains coat-related terminology and where coat tests fit in the larger decision;
- both articles link to each other contextually;
- neither article claims that a color panel is a complete health panel.

No existing local article has the same primary intent. The topic is therefore not a duplicate of
the seven currently registered local posts.

## Source hierarchy

Use sources in this order:

1. The current French Bull Dog Club of America breed standard for what is acceptable in the
   American conformation standard.
2. University veterinary genetics laboratories for gene, locus, inheritance, and test
   interpretation.
3. OFA/CHIC and the breed club for health-screening requirements and limitations.
4. Peer-reviewed research for specific health associations and uncertainties.
5. AKC for registration terminology and its own French Bulldog test information.
6. The breeder's verified first-hand experience and original photographs, clearly labeled as
   experience rather than universal medical evidence.

Competitor breeder pages may reveal common vocabulary, but they must not be used as the factual
authority. Do not copy their color descriptions, allele charts, health claims, or photography.

## What the authoritative sources establish

### 1. Breed-standard color and registration color are different questions

The current [FBDCA French Bulldog breed standard](https://frenchbulldogclub.org/breedstandard/)
accepts white, cream, fawn from light to red, or combinations of those colors. It describes
brindle, piebald, black masks, black shadings, and white markings as accepted patterns or
markings. It lists all other colors or patterns as disqualifications and gives examples including
solid black, black and tan, black and white, white with black, blue, blue fawn, liver, and merle.

The standard is a conformation standard: it describes the ideal against which dogs are judged. It
is not a laboratory health report, and conformity to a standard color does not guarantee health.

[AKC's conformation FAQ](https://www.akc.org/sports/conformation/resources/faq/) explicitly says
that some colors may be disqualifications in a breed standard while dogs of those colors may still
be registered. The ability to record a color during registration is therefore not the same thing
as that color being accepted by the breed standard. The article should explain this without
suggesting that registration is fake or that conformation status is a medical diagnosis.

**Safe buyer wording**

```text
“AKC registered” answers a registration question. “Standard color” answers a conformation-standard
question. Neither statement, by itself, tells you whether the parents completed the recommended
health screenings.
```

### 2. Coat color is produced by interacting genes, not a single color switch

The [UC Davis Veterinary Genetics Laboratory French Bulldog panel](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/panel/dog-coat-color-french-bulldog)
describes multiple interacting loci. A buyer-friendly article does not need a complete breeding
calculator, but it should explain the following roles:

| Buyer-facing layer | Gene/locus example | Simplified role                                                              | Important limitation                                                           |
| ------------------ | ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Pigment switching  | `ASIP` / A locus   | Helps distribute red/yellow and black/brown pigment                          | Expression depends on other loci                                               |
| Dominant black     | `CBD103` / K locus | Influences whether eumelanin dominates or an agouti pattern can show         | Must be interpreted with A and E loci                                          |
| Mask/red/cream     | `MC1R` / E locus   | Influences mask and whether dark pigment is expressed in the coat            | Multiple alleles and interactions exist                                        |
| Brown              | `TYRP1` / B locus  | Changes black eumelanin toward brown                                         | Does not simply lighten red/yellow pigment                                     |
| Cocoa              | `HPS3` / Co locus  | A separate recessive cause of brown coat in French Bulldogs                  | HPS3 alone cannot predict the entire coat                                      |
| Dilute/blue        | `MLPH` / D locus   | Dilutes eumelanin to paler shades                                            | More than one dilution variant exists                                          |
| White spotting     | `MITF` / S locus   | Contributes to piebald or white spotting                                     | Extent and pattern are variable and genetically complex                        |
| Merle              | `PMEL`             | Produces irregular areas of diluted and solid pigment                        | Allele size, mosaicism, and hidden expression complicate visual classification |
| Coat length        | `FGF5` / L locus   | Recessive variants can produce long hair, called `fluffy` in French Bulldogs | This is coat type, not coat color                                              |
| Pigment intensity  | several loci       | Helps explain lighter or deeper red/yellow shades                            | Intensity is not accurately reduced to one universal switch                    |

The [UC Davis coat-length test](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/coat-length-dog) identifies five
recessive `FGF5` variants and specifically notes that long hair is also called `fluffy` in French
Bulldogs. The FBDCA standard describes the coat as short and smooth and disqualifies other coat
types. Therefore `fluffy` belongs in a coat-type sidebar, not in a list of colors.

Research on pigment intensity reinforces the need for modest claims. A peer-reviewed study found
that five loci explained over 70%—not 100%—of pheomelanin intensity variation in its independent
validation dataset. See
[Slavney et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34043658/).

### 3. A visual label is not always a complete genotype

Two dogs can look similar while carrying different combinations, and a dog can carry a recessive
variant without visually expressing it. Testing terminology also changes as new variants are
identified. For example, a third `MLPH` dilution allele was reported after earlier D-locus tests
already existed. See
[Minor et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32531980/).

The safe editorial lesson is not that testing is unreliable. It is that the report must identify
which variants were tested, and its result must be interpreted within the scope of that test.

**Do not write**

```text
One photo or one locus proves the dog's complete color genetics.
```

**Write instead**

```text
A laboratory report can identify the variants included in that test. It may confirm important
parts of the coat genotype, but appearance and a single result do not always explain every shade
or pattern.
```

### 4. Color testing and health testing are separate products

The [AKC French Bulldog DNA testing page](https://www.akc.org/breeder-programs/dna/dna-resource-center/akc-dna-health/breed-specific-dna-testing-recommendations/french-bulldog-dna-tests/)
separates coat-color and trait loci from health variants and from the breed club's recommended
screenings. It lists patella, hip, congenital cardiac, and ophthalmologist evaluations as the
recommended FBDCA screening categories, with juvenile cataract DNA and thyroid listed as optional.

The [OFA CHIC program](https://ofa.org/chic-programs/) states that a CHIC number means the dog was
screened for the breed-specific required categories and the results are publicly available. A
CHIC number is evidence of testing transparency, but it does not mean every result was normal and
is not a lifetime guarantee.

This distinction should become one of the strongest visual blocks in the article:

| Color/trait panel can help answer                        | Health review must answer separately                         |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Which tested coat variants are present?                  | Were the breed-club screening categories completed?          |
| Is the dog a carrier for a tested recessive coat trait?  | What were the actual hip, patella, cardiac, and eye results? |
| Can the tested variants help explain a pattern or shade? | What puppy examination and veterinary records are available? |
| Does the report include merle or long-hair testing?      | What conditions are not covered by the selected DNA panel?   |

### 5. Merle requires a specific, careful explanation

The [UC Davis merle resource](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/test/merle) explains that merle is associated
with a `PMEL` SINE insertion and is incompletely dominant. Expression exists on a continuum;
cryptic merle may show little or no visible pattern, and mosaic results can occur. This is why a
visual guess is not a responsible breeding tool where merle is present.

UC Davis warns that breeding two dogs that possess merle variants can produce double-merle
offspring and that double-merle dogs may have auditory, ophthalmologic, skeletal, and other
defects. A peer-reviewed BAER study across merle breeds found substantially higher deafness rates
in double-merle than single-merle dogs, but its figures are not French Bulldog-specific. See
[Strain et al., 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19192156/).

The article must avoid converting cross-breed data into a precise French Bulldog risk percentage.
It should also avoid implying that every single-merle dog is clinically affected. The useful
buyer question is whether the relevant dogs were genetically tested and how the pairing avoided
a merle-to-merle combination.

### 6. Dilution and color-dilution alopecia are related, but not identical

Variants in `MLPH` are associated with dilute coat color. Color-dilution alopecia is a recognized
skin disorder reported in dilute dogs, but not every dog with a dilute genotype develops the
condition. Much of the published incidence information is breed-specific and cannot simply be
transferred to French Bulldogs.

Primary starting points:

- [Drögemüller et al., 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15960853/) links `MLPH` variants with
  dilute coat color.
- [Caramalc et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34088257/) discusses molecular
  confirmation of color-dilution alopecia in affected dogs from several other breeds.
- [Minor et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32531980/) shows that D-locus testing must
  account for multiple dilution alleles.

**Safe wording**

```text
Dilute pigmentation is associated with color-dilution alopecia in some dogs, but a dilute result
does not mean that every dog will develop hair loss. Ask about the parents' skin history, the
specific variants tested, and veterinary findings rather than accepting either a guarantee or a
blanket diagnosis from color alone.
```

### 7. Cocoa is a good example of why uncertainty must be shown honestly

A nonsense variant in `HPS3` has been identified as a cause of cocoa color in French Bulldogs.
Research on a small French Bulldog group found changes in platelet dense granules but no evidence
of major health problems or owner-reported increased bleeding in that sample. The authors still
said further research was needed to rule out subtle visual or clinically relevant bleeding
effects. See
[Letko et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8304396/).

This is not evidence for either `cocoa is dangerous` or `cocoa is proven harmless`. The article
should use cocoa as a brief example of responsible uncertainty:

```text
The color-causing variant is known; the complete long-term clinical meaning is not settled by a
single small study.
```

The same study also found that testing `HPS3` alone cannot reliably predict the dog's entire coat
because other color loci interact with it.

### 8. White spotting and hearing must be handled at the correct level of certainty

Veterinary literature associates congenital sensorineural deafness in several dog breeds with
white-pigmentation pathways, including piebald and merle. The mechanism and prevalence differ by
breed, and the reviewed evidence does not justify telling a French Bulldog buyer that every white
or piebald dog is deaf or high-risk.

Use the general association only if the final article makes the breed limitation explicit. See
[Strain, 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10390790/) and the later
[review of deafness genetics in domestic animals](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4672198/).

If the breeder has documented BAER testing, that can be described accurately. Do not imply BAER
testing is performed routinely unless the owner confirms it for the relevant dog or litter.

## Claim-safety matrix

| Tempting claim                                            | Decision | Publishable correction                                                                                                                      |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | -------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `Rare colors are unhealthy.`                              | Reject   | Some pigment variants or pairings have documented risks; health must be evaluated from the specific genotype, pairing, screenings, and dog. |
| `Standard colors are the healthiest.`                     | Reject   | Standard color is a conformation classification, not a health guarantee.                                                                    |
| `AKC registered means AKC standard color.`                | Reject   | Registration and conformation-standard acceptance are separate questions.                                                                   |
| `A clear color panel means a healthy puppy.`              | Reject   | A coat panel answers coat-trait questions; breed-specific health screening is separate.                                                     |
| `Merle always causes blindness or deafness.`              | Reject   | Risk is most clearly established for double-merle combinations; expression and health outcomes vary.                                        |
| `A merle-looking dog can be paired safely by appearance.` | Reject   | Cryptic merle and mosaicism make DNA testing important when merle is present.                                                               |
| `Every blue dog gets color-dilution alopecia.`            | Reject   | CDA occurs in a subset of dilute dogs and prevalence varies by breed.                                                                       |
| `Cocoa has no health implications.`                       | Reject   | A small study did not find major clinical problems but did find a platelet-related effect and called for further research.                  |
| `Fluffy is a rare color.`                                 | Reject   | Fluffy describes long coat associated with recessive `FGF5` variants, not a color.                                                          |
| `DNA can predict the exact final shade.`                  | Reject   | Multiple loci, interactions, test scope, and phenotype variation limit exact prediction.                                                    |

## Original article concept: The Four-Layer Color Decoder

### Opening scene

Start with a buyer reading a fictional listing that sounds like a password:

```text
“Lilac-and-tan fluffy carrier, testable cocoa, no pied.”
```

The point is not to ridicule the vocabulary. The point is to show why a buyer can receive a long
color description and still know almost nothing about the health screenings, parents, or puppy.

Then introduce the decoder:

1. **What your eyes see — phenotype.** The visible coat, pattern, nose pigment, and hair length.
2. **What the laboratory found — genotype.** Only the variants included in the selected test.
3. **What the listing calls it — label.** A mix of standard terminology, breeder shorthand, and
   marketing language.
4. **What protects the puppy — evidence.** Parent screening, the actual reports, veterinary care,
   pairing decisions, puppy records, and contract.

This structure is more useful and memorable than an alphabetized allele glossary.

### Proposed article flow

#### H2: The Color Label That Sounds Like a Password

- use the short opening scene;
- tell the buyer they do not need to become a geneticist;
- promise a practical method for reading claims and documents.

#### H2: First, Separate Four Different Questions

- render the four-layer decoder as four compact cards;
- show one worked example without identifying a real breeder or puppy;
- make `appearance is not a complete health record` the takeaway.

#### H2: The Two Pigments Behind the Palette

- explain eumelanin as the dark-pigment family and pheomelanin as the red/yellow family;
- explain that genes control production, placement, dilution, white spotting, and coat type;
- avoid a chemistry lecture and do not imply two pigments mean only two genes.

#### H2: A Buyer-Friendly Map of the Main Loci

- use the simplified table from this brief;
- add an optional `Show the genetics details` disclosure for readers who want allele notation;
- keep mobile cells short and avoid a wall of genotype codes.

#### H2: Standard, Registrable, and Marketed Are Not the Same Thing

- quote no more than necessary from the FBDCA standard; paraphrase and link to it;
- list accepted standard groups and examples of disqualifying groups;
- explain that conformation status does not equal health status;
- explain that an AKC registration entry does not certify all buyer claims.

#### H2: Three Places Where Color and Health Need More Care

Use three `What we know / What we cannot conclude` cards:

1. Merle and especially merle-to-merle pairings.
2. Dilution and color-dilution alopecia.
3. White spotting/hearing, with the breed-specific evidence limitation.

Add cocoa as a smaller `Research is still developing` sidebar rather than presenting it as a
fourth proven disease association.

#### H2: What a Color DNA Report Can—and Cannot—Tell You

- identify the dog, laboratory, report date, and variants tested;
- distinguish `not detected`, carrier, and expressed genotypes where relevant;
- explain that incomplete panels and newly characterized variants affect interpretation;
- state that the report is not a lifetime warranty or substitute for clinical screening.

#### H2: Ask for the Color Receipt

Turn the buyer checklist into a visual receipt rather than another generic bullet list:

```text
COLOR RECEIPT
[ ] The puppy/dog identity matches the report
[ ] Laboratory and report date are visible
[ ] Tested loci are listed—not just “DNA tested”
[ ] Both parents' relevant color results are available
[ ] The pairing explanation is clear, especially for merle
[ ] Breed-specific health screenings are shown separately
[ ] Actual results—not only a CHIC badge or certificate—were reviewed
[ ] Puppy veterinary records and contract are available
[ ] Price is not justified only by the word “rare”
```

#### H2: Health Before Color: A 60-Second Decision Path

Use a compact decision tree:

```text
Do I like this puppy's color?
        ↓
Can the seller document the color claim?
        ↓
Can the seller separately document parent health screening and puppy veterinary care?
        ↓
Does the pairing avoid a known high-risk color combination?
        ↓
Do temperament, structure, contract, support, and family fit also make sense?
```

Color can remain part of preference, but it never gets to skip the evidence steps.

#### H2: Questions Buyers Commonly Ask

Keep answers concise and derived from the main article:

1. What colors are accepted by the French Bulldog breed standard?
2. Can a non-standard-color French Bulldog be AKC registered?
3. Are rare-colored French Bulldogs less healthy?
4. What is the difference between color testing and health testing?
5. Is fluffy a color?
6. What should I ask before buying a merle French Bulldog?

FAQ structured data may be included for semantic consistency, but no rich-result promise should
be made.

## Sample opening in the intended voice

> You open a puppy listing and find a label long enough to need punctuation: “lilac-and-tan
> fluffy carrier, testable cocoa, no pied.” It sounds precise. It may even be based on a real DNA
> report. But it still leaves the most important buyer questions unanswered.
>
> What does the puppy actually look like? Which variants did the laboratory test? Is the label a
> breed-standard term or seller shorthand? And where are the hip, patella, cardiac, eye, and puppy
> veterinary records?
>
> The easiest way to understand French Bulldog color genetics is not to memorize every letter. It
> is to sort every claim into four layers: what you can see, what the lab detected, what the listing
> calls it, and what the health evidence proves.

This opening is original to this project. The final draft should preserve its conversational
clarity while avoiding jokes that belittle owners, breeders, or dogs of any color.

## Visual and interaction recommendation

The final page should be visually lighter than a long static genetics article:

- a four-card decoder near the top;
- one compact, horizontally scrollable locus table on small screens;
- `What we know / What we cannot conclude` cards for health associations;
- an expandable genetics-details area rather than exposing every allele by default;
- the Color Receipt checklist;
- the final decision path;
- no large blocks of decorative emoji;
- no quiz that declares a puppy healthy or unhealthy from color answers.

If interactivity is added, it should be educational only. A user may select a term such as `blue`,
`merle`, or `fluffy` to reveal its category, likely relevant test, standard status, and the next
document to request. It must display a clear notice that it does not diagnose a dog or validate a
breeding pair.

## Original evidence needed from Exotic Bulldog Legacy

Before drafting the final article, request the following from the owner:

- which color/trait laboratory is used and a redacted sample report;
- which loci are actually included in the breeder's current panel;
- whether merle is present in any current or planned line and, if so, how pairing results are
  documented;
- two to six original dog photographs whose color labels are verified by a report;
- permission to show a redacted report screenshot;
- which FBDCA/OFA/CHIC screening records are available for current breeding dogs;
- a short first-person explanation of how health, temperament, structure, and color are weighted
  in a pairing decision;
- whether a veterinarian or qualified canine geneticist will review the medical/genetics language.

Do not infer any of these facts from puppy photographs or previous marketing copy.

## Final drafting guardrails

- Never diagnose a dog, pairing, or breeder from a coat label.
- Never state that a color causes a condition unless the cited evidence supports that exact
  relationship and population.
- State clearly when evidence comes from multiple breeds rather than French Bulldogs.
- Distinguish association, predisposition, carrier status, and certainty.
- Do not promise that a standard-colored or health-tested dog will never develop disease.
- Do not describe CHIC as `all clear`; inspect the underlying public results.
- Do not call a puppy `rare` as an objective genetic category without defining the dataset and
  population.
- Do not convert a competitor's price premium into evidence of rarity, quality, or health.
- Do not publish an allele-pairing calculator without review by a qualified canine geneticist.
- Include a veterinary-information disclaimer, but do not use the disclaimer to excuse
  unsupported medical claims.
- Add a visible author and `reviewed/updated` date. Do not invent a veterinary reviewer.
