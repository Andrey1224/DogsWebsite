# 2. Готовый текст статьи

# How to Choose a Healthy Bulldog Puppy: DNA Health Tests & Vet Checkup Guide

Bringing home a French or English Bulldog puppy is an exciting milestone.

It is easy to fall in love with the wrinkles, expressive eyes, bat ears, compact body, or a beautiful coat color. But appearance should never be the only thing you evaluate when choosing a puppy.

Behind every healthy puppy should be something much more important:

**thoughtful breeding, documented health screening, veterinary care, and transparency from the breeder.**

You do not need a veterinary degree or an advanced understanding of canine genetics to ask the right questions.

You simply need to know **what documentation to request and what a responsible breeder should be willing to discuss with you.**

> **Our Health Philosophy**
>
> At Exotic Bulldog Legacy, we believe buyers should understand the health background of the puppy they are considering. Ask questions, review the available records for the specific litter, and understand what veterinary care your puppy has received before going home.

---

## Start With the Parents, Not Just the Puppy

A puppy's health story begins before the puppy is born.

Genetic screening of breeding dogs can help breeders identify certain inherited mutations and make more informed decisions when selecting a sire and dam.

DNA testing cannot predict every health problem a dog may ever develop. But for conditions with established genetic tests, it provides valuable information about the parents and potential risks to their offspring.

OFA specifically recommends that prospective puppy buyers research health testing on parents and relatives when evaluating a breeder.

### Ask to See the Actual Results

Do not be afraid to ask:

**“What health testing was performed on the sire and dam of this litter, and can I see the results?”**

A breeder should be able to explain what testing was performed for that specific pairing and provide the available documentation.

Do not rely only on phrases such as:

**“health tested,” “DNA clear,” or “vet checked.”**

Ask what was actually tested.

---

## Understanding DNA Test Results

Laboratories may use slightly different terminology, but DNA reports commonly classify a dog according to whether a disease-associated variant was detected.

For many **autosomal recessive conditions**, the basic interpretation looks like this:

### Clear / Non-Carrier

The tested mutation was not detected.

For that specific mutation, the dog is not expected to pass a disease-associated copy to its offspring.

### Carrier

One copy of the tested mutation was detected.

For many recessive conditions, carriers themselves do not develop the disease associated with two copies. Responsible breeding decisions depend on the inheritance pattern and the genetic status of the mate.

For example, pairing a carrier with a genetically clear dog can prevent puppies from inheriting two copies of that particular recessive mutation.

### At Risk / Affected

The laboratory has detected the disease-associated genotype corresponding to increased risk or affected status for that particular test.

The exact meaning depends on the disease and inheritance pattern, so the laboratory's interpretation should always be reviewed rather than assuming every genetic test works identically.

> **Key Idea**
>
> A DNA result is not simply a “good dog / bad dog” label. The purpose of testing is to give breeders information they can use to make better pairing decisions.

---

# DNA Health Tests Commonly Seen in French Bulldogs

The exact testing panel depends on the laboratory and breeding program.

Current French Bulldog panels offered by Animal Genetics include tests for conditions such as **CMR1, Degenerative Myelopathy, Hyperuricosuria, Juvenile Hereditary Cataracts, and Cystinuria variants**, among others.

Here are several names buyers may encounter on health reports.

## Degenerative Myelopathy — DM

DM is associated with progressive degeneration of the spinal cord.

DNA testing identifies variants associated with increased genetic risk, helping breeders make more informed breeding decisions.

---

## Juvenile Hereditary Cataracts — JHC

JHC is an inherited form of cataract that can affect younger dogs.

Testing helps identify dogs carrying the known mutation associated with this condition.

---

## Canine Multifocal Retinopathy Type 1 — CMR1

CMR1 is an inherited retinal condition.

DNA screening can identify the associated mutation and help breeders reduce the likelihood of producing genetically affected puppies.

---

## Hyperuricosuria — HUU

HUU affects uric acid metabolism and can increase the risk of urate stones in the urinary system.

Identifying the genetic status of breeding dogs allows breeders to plan pairings more carefully.

---

## Cystinuria

Certain forms of cystinuria are associated with abnormal cystine levels in the urine and the development of urinary stones.

Some French Bulldog panels include specific Cystinuria Type 3 variants.

---

# DNA Health Tests Commonly Seen in English Bulldogs

English Bulldog panels may include several overlapping conditions.

Animal Genetics currently lists **CMR1, Cystinuria Type 3 variants, DM, and HUU** among the disease-related tests included in its English Bulldog panel.

The important point for buyers is not memorizing every abbreviation.

It is understanding that genetic testing should be **specific, documented, and interpreted in the context of the actual breeding pair.**

---

# DNA Testing Is Only One Part of Bulldog Health

One of the biggest mistakes buyers can make is assuming that a DNA panel means:

**“This puppy can never have a health problem.”**

That is not what genetic testing means.

Not every health condition has a DNA test. Some Bulldog health concerns are evaluated through physical examination, specialist screening, imaging, respiratory assessment, orthopedic evaluation, or other forms of health testing.

The OFA/CHIC system specifically distinguishes DNA-based testing from phenotype-based health evaluations and recommends breed-specific screening protocols.

For brachycephalic breeds such as Bulldogs and French Bulldogs, respiratory function is also an important consideration. OFA currently operates its Respiratory Function Grading Scheme for Bulldogs and French Bulldogs.

That is why choosing a puppy should involve **both the parents' health background and the puppy's own veterinary examination.**

---

# The Pre-Sale Veterinary Checkup

Before going to a new home, a puppy should receive an age-appropriate veterinary evaluation.

Documentation may look different from clinic to clinic. Some veterinarians provide a detailed examination sheet, others use medical-record notes or a health certificate.

What matters is that there is a documented veterinary assessment of the puppy.

Your current Exotic Bulldog Legacy health policy already states that puppies receive a comprehensive veterinary exam, age-appropriate vaccinations, and health records before transfer.

# Vaccinations and Deworming Records

Do not accept:

**“Yes, the puppy has had shots.”**

Ask to see the record.

The puppy's medical documentation should clearly show what veterinary care has been administered and when.

Age-appropriate vaccinations and deworming should be documented so your veterinarian can continue the schedule without guessing or unnecessarily repeating treatment.

Your site's current FAQ already tells buyers that puppies go home with veterinary health and vaccination records and a deworming record.

---

# What About a Fecal Test?

Young puppies can carry intestinal parasites even when they look completely healthy.

A fecal examination may be used to screen for parasites such as worms, Giardia, or coccidia, depending on the veterinary protocol and puppy history.

Ask the breeder what parasite prevention and fecal screening have been performed and what the records show.

---

# Look at the Puppy, Too

Documents matter, but buyers should also observe the puppy.

A healthy puppy should generally appear alert, comfortable, curious, and appropriately active for its age and situation.

Pay attention to the puppy's:

**breathing, movement, eyes, skin, overall body condition, behavior, and interaction with people.**

Bulldog puppies can vary significantly in personality, so quiet does not automatically mean unhealthy and energetic does not automatically mean healthy.

The goal is to look at the complete picture.

# Health Does Not Stop at Pickup

Choosing a thoughtfully bred and properly examined puppy gives you a stronger starting point.

What happens afterward matters too.

### Protect Developing Joints

Young Bulldogs should not repeatedly jump from high furniture or run up and down steep stairs.

Their growing bodies need time to mature.

### Maintain a Healthy Body Weight

Excess body weight increases physical stress and is especially undesirable in brachycephalic breeds.

Keeping your Bulldog lean and appropriately conditioned supports mobility and overall health.

### Protect Bulldogs From Heat

French and English Bulldogs are brachycephalic breeds and can have more difficulty dissipating heat than longer-muzzled dogs.

Avoid prolonged exercise during hot conditions, provide shade and water, and learn the early signs of heat stress.

### Continue Veterinary Care

The breeder's veterinary exam is the beginning, not the end.

Schedule your own veterinarian visit after bringing the puppy home according to the requirements in your contract and your veterinarian's recommendation.
